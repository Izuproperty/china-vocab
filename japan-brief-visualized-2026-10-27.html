STORY 1 · AI & TECH
Sakana AI Will Pretrain Its Own Japanese Model, Sawara, After Two Years of Building on Other Labs' Weights

サカナAI (Sakana Ē-Ai, Sakana AI) said on October 26 that it will train a Japanese 基盤モデル (kiban moderu, foundation model) from randomly initialized weights, its first attempt at full 事前学習 (jizen gakushū, pre-training). CEO David Ha announced the project, tentatively named Sawara, at a symposium at Hitotsubashi Hall in Tokyo. Development starts in November, a smaller model is due in spring 2027, and a larger version aimed at hundreds of billions of parameters follows. Chief Scientist Takuya Akiba will lead a team of about 20 that uses AI agents to help run training experiments, and Jürgen Schmidhuber, a pioneer of recurrent neural networks, gave the keynote as Sakana's new chief scientific advisor. Sakana has not disclosed the compute, training data or budget.


The plan reverses the approach Sakana has used since 2023, when it began building products by merging, fine-tuning and orchestrating other labs' open models. Its commercial Namazu model is post-trained on Kimi K2.6, an open-weight model from China's Moonshot AI, and its Fugu orchestrator routes tasks across US models. That works for general business use but runs into trouble with the customers Sakana now depends on: the Ministry of Defense, government cyber teams, MUFG, SMBC and Daiwa Securities. These buyers want to know what data went into a model and need assurance that a foreign supplier cannot change or withdraw it. A 国産 (kokusan, domestically built) base checkpoint answers both demands, and owning the weights lets Sakana choose its own data mixture.


Compute is the constraint. Training a model with hundreds of billions of parameters from scratch typically takes thousands of GPUs for months, and Sakana's last round raised ¥32 billion in November 2025 at a ¥432 billion valuation, which covers only part of that bill. The state-backed Noetra consortium already holds ¥387.3 billion of METI money for fiscal 2026 to build a sovereign multimodal model by 2028, and MUFG backs both efforts. Sakana will therefore compete with its own shareholder's other bet for government compute and procurement. The name keeps Sakana's fish theme: sawara is Spanish mackerel.

Investor takeaway: Sakana is private, so listed exposure runs through its backers and suppliers: MUFG (TSE: 8306), Mitsubishi Electric (TSE: 6503) and domestic GPU clouds such as Sakura Internet (TSE: 3778). Pretraining from scratch raises Sakana's cash burn and makes a new funding round likely in 2027. Watch for a named compute partner and for direct Ministry of Defense funding. Without either, the spring 2027 small model has to prove that a 20-person team can match the open-weight models Sakana used to build on.

STORY 2 · SEMICONDUCTORS
Nikon Takes Orders for the First Submicron Display Lithography Tool and Aims It at Korean and Chinese OLED Makers

ニコン (Nikon, Nikon) began taking orders on October 26 for the FX-6ASLH, a flat panel display 露光装置 (rokō sōchi, lithography system) that prints circuit lines 0.85 micrometers wide on glass. Nikon says it is the first FPD tool to resolve below 1 micrometer. It reaches 1.0 µm contact holes and an overlay accuracy, or 重ね合わせ精度 (kasaneawase seido, overlay accuracy), of ±0.18 µm or better, works on G6 glass of 1,500 by 1,850 millimeters, and exposes a plate every 39 seconds using a UV-LED light source. The finest lines require a phase-shift mask and chemically amplified resist. Sales start in December 2027, and a Nikon official said the company will focus on display makers in South Korea and China.


The tool patterns the thin-film transistor layers behind high-resolution 有機EL (yūki EL, OLED) panels for phones and foldables. Those panels need finer wiring as pixel density rises and as makers pack more circuitry into narrower bezels, and Nikon says G6 lines for small and medium panels increasingly need resolution below 1 µm. Nikon and Canon are the two main suppliers of FPD lithography, and Korean and Chinese makers such as Samsung Display and BOE run most of the world's G6 OLED capacity, so that is where the orders will come from.


The same large-glass optics carry Nikon into chip packaging. In June Nikon said it is developing a maskless digital lithography system with 1.5 µm 解像度 (kaizōdo, resolution) and at least 65 panels an hour for semiconductor advanced packaging, due in fiscal 2027, and it has taken orders since July 2025 for the 1.0 µm DSP-100. Panel-level packaging for AI chips uses large rectangular substrates like display glass, and Nikon built the DSP-100 by combining its FPD multi-lens optics with its semiconductor lithography technology. Nikon lost front-end chip lithography to ASML two decades ago, and these back-end tools are how it gets a share of AI chip spending again.

Investor takeaway: Nikon (TSE: 7731) books no revenue from the FX-6ASLH until late 2027, so near-term numbers do not change. The order book will show whether Samsung Display and BOE commit to upgrading G6 OLED lines for higher-resolution phone panels, and a slowdown in Chinese display capex is the main risk. Watch Nikon's next results for FPD order intake and first DSP-100 shipments, which would show whether its packaging tools are turning into chip-related revenue.

STORY 3 · FINANCE
Meiji Yasuda Buys About 5% of Man Group and Pledges ¥200 Billion to Its Private Credit Funds Over Three Years

明治安田生命 (Meiji Yasuda Seimei, Meiji Yasuda Life Insurance) said on October 26 that it will buy about 5% of Man Group, the London-listed hedge fund and credit manager, through open-market purchases, and will place ¥200 billion (about $1.3 billion) with Man Group over three years, mostly in プライベートクレジット (puraibēto kurejitto, private credit). The stake was worth about £179 million at the October 23 close, and Man Group will issue no new shares. Meiji Yasuda accepted a three-year lock-up and a standstill that caps its holding at 19.99%. The two firms will develop products for Japanese clients together, and Man Group becomes a preferred asset manager for the insurer. Man Group shares rose as much as 4.9% in London.


This is Meiji Yasuda's first major investment in an overseas asset manager, and it extends a partnership signed in February 2025. The insurer is a 相互会社 (sōgo gaisha, mutual company) owned by its policyholders, so it cannot issue shares to fund a large acquisition. A minority stake plus a mandate is a cheaper way into foreign 資産運用 (shisan un'yō, asset management). Its peers have spent far more: Nippon Life bought Resolution Life for $8.2 billion, and Dai-ichi Life offered A$7.9 billion for Australia's Challenger on October 18, an offer Challenger's board rejected on October 26. The Man Group money is part of Meiji Yasuda's plan to put ¥600 billion into private assets through fiscal 2027.


The deal sends money abroad while domestic yields are the highest in decades. The 10-year JGB yield is near 2.8% and the 20-year near 3.6%, and Japanese life insurers have been buying long JGBs to match their liabilities under the economic-value solvency rules that took effect for fiscal 2025. Meiji Yasuda is still adding overseas credit because private loans pay a spread that JGBs do not, and because Man Group brings origination and quantitative skills the insurer lacks in-house. Those solvency rules also mark holdings to economic value, so a downturn in private credit, where industry warnings about loose underwriting are growing, would hit reported capital quickly.

Investor takeaway: Man Group gets ¥200 billion of long-term insurance money and a Japanese distribution partner. For Japanese markets, the deal shows that higher JGB yields have not stopped the big life insurers from adding overseas credit, which limits how much repatriation demand the bond market should expect from them. Watch the insurers' second-half investment plans, which they usually brief in late October, and whether Dai-ichi raises its Challenger price.
