STORY 1 · AI & TECH
Fujitsu Signs a Three-Year Deal to Open a Quantum Research Center in New Mexico

富士通 (Fujitsū, Fujitsu) said on October 9 that it will set up a quantum computing research center in the US state of New Mexico. Governor Michelle Lujan Grisham and Fujitsu CTO Vivek Mahajan signed the three-year memorandum at the state Capitol in Santa Fe on October 8, local time. The center will work on quantum hardware, software, algorithms and theory and will run internships and workforce programs. Its partners are the Quantum New Mexico Institute and New Mexico State University, which already works with Fujitsu on the FUJITSU-MONAKA CPU, and the parties will explore joint research with Sandia and Los Alamos national laboratories. The agreement carries no financial commitment. The state's economic development department will tell Fujitsu which incentives it qualifies for, and Mahajan called the signing the first step in a "long-term decision" to put resources into quantum.


New Mexico is paying to build a quantum cluster. Lawmakers appropriated more than $300 million for quantum companies and workforce in the current budget year, and key legislators say state support could reach $1.5 billion over several years. A $25 million state-funded quantum lab recently opened in downtown Albuquerque. The US Department of Defense announced a $350 million quantum computing initiative the same day. Fujitsu's home program runs with 理化学研究所 (Rikagaku Kenkyūsho, RIKEN). The two unveiled a 256-qubit 超伝導量子コンピュータ (chōdendō ryōshi konpyūta, superconducting quantum computer) in April 2025, plan a 1,000-qubit machine in fiscal 2026, and target a system with more than 10,000 physical qubits and 250 論理量子ビット (ronri ryōshi bitto, logical qubits) by fiscal 2030.

Investor takeaway: The memorandum costs Fujitsu little and opens US national labs and New Mexico's quantum funding to its hardware and MONAKA servers. It will not move earnings for years. A named site or a Fujitsu spending figure would show the partnership has substance.

STORY 2 · SEMI & INDUSTRIAL
Kyocera Wins a ¥3 Billion Subsidy to Recycle Tungsten From Used Tools After China Stops Shipping to Japan

京セラ (Kyōsera, Kyocera) said on October 9 that it will build a smelting line at its 鹿児島川内工場 (Kagoshima Sendai Kōjō, Kagoshima Sendai plant) in Satsumasendai to recover 炭化タングステン (tanka tangusuten, tungsten carbide) from used cutting tools, with operations starting in fiscal 2030. 経済産業省 (Keizai Sangyōshō, METI) certified the project under its supply-security program for 重要鉱物 (jūyō kōbutsu, critical minerals), and Jiji reports that Kyocera will receive a ¥3 billion subsidy. The line uses a Kyocera process based on naturally derived amino acids that removes steps from conventional recycling. Kyocera did not disclose capacity or total cost.


Tungsten carbide is the hard phase in 超硬合金 (chōkō gōkin, cemented carbide), the material in most metal-cutting inserts and end mills. Tungsten also enters chipmaking as tungsten hexafluoride gas, which deposits the metal contacts in advanced logic chips. China mines more than 80% of world supply. After Beijing tightened export controls, Chinese customs data showed zero shipments of tungsten carbide and tungsten powder to Japan from February through April. Sumitomo Electric said in May that its Chinese procurement had stopped and raised tool prices by up to 60%. 三菱マテリアル (Mitsubishi Materiaru, Mitsubishi Materials) more than tripled cemented carbide prices on orders from June, and Nikkei reported on October 9 that it is buying recycled tungsten from Vietnam. Japan recycles about 30% of the tungsten it uses.

Investor takeaway: Japan's response to China's tungsten cutoff is recycling, and Kyocera's line starts in fiscal 2030. Until then carbide tool prices stay high, which helps tool makers with scrap supply and hurts machine shops and parts makers. Chinese export licenses for Japan are the main risk to that pricing.

STORY 3 · FINANCE
The FSA Stops New Sales at Prudential Life and Gibraltar Life From Tuesday Over ¥5.2 Billion in Employee Fraud

金融庁 (Kin'yūchō, FSA) on October 9 ordered プルデンシャル生命保険 (Purudensharu Seimei Hoken, Prudential Life Insurance) and group company ジブラルタ生命保険 (Jiburaruta Seimei Hoken, Gibraltar Life Insurance) to stop soliciting and underwriting new policies from October 13 through January 31 under an 一部業務停止命令 (ichibu gyōmu teishi meirei, partial business suspension order). Both insurers and their holding company, Prudential Holdings of Japan, also received 業務改善命令 (gyōmu kaizen meirei, business improvement orders). Gibraltar's sales through outside agencies are exempt, and existing policies will be serviced and claims paid. Improvement plans are due by November 30, with progress reports every three months after that. The companies belong to US-based Prudential Financial.


A third-party committee reported on October 8 that more than 100 current and former salespeople took money from customers in fraudulent or improper deals worth about ¥5.2 billion over more than three decades. The FSA blamed the pay system. About three quarters of sales pay came from commissions, staff from their third year received no fixed salary, and the regulator called that 完全歩合制 (kanzen buaisei, full-commission pay) structure a breeding ground for misconduct. Prudential Life had already stopped new sales voluntarily on February 9, and new policy sales fell 90.7% year on year in the April-June quarter. In April, Prudential Financial estimated that a full-year halt would cut its 2026 pre-tax adjusted operating income by $525-575 million, and it withdrew its 5-8% medium-term EPS growth target.

Investor takeaway: The suspension extends a sales halt that began in February, so most of the 2026 hit to Prudential's Japan earnings is already known. Resumption in February is not guaranteed. By naming full-commission pay as a cause, the FSA puts sales incentives across the life insurance industry under scrutiny, while listed rivals with agency and bank channels gain share.
