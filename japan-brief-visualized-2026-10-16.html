STORY 1 · AI & TECH
NICT, PFN and Sakura Internet Will Run a National AI Safety Test That Writes Its Own Prompts, Starting Next Fiscal Year

情報通信研究機構 (Jōhō Tsūshin Kenkyū Kikō, National Institute of Information and Communications Technology, NICT), プリファードネットワークス (Purifādo Nettowākusu, Preferred Networks, PFN) and さくらインターネット (Sakura Intānetto, Sakura Internet) said on October 14 that they will put NICT's 能動的評価基盤 (nōdōteki hyōka kiban, active evaluation platform) into service in the fiscal year that starts in April 2027. The platform handles 安全性評価 (anzensei hyōka, safety evaluation) of generative AI by writing new test prompts continuously. Most current safety tests score a model against a fixed prompt database. Developers can train on those prompts and pass without becoming safer, and a fixed set cannot catch fraud techniques or misuse that appear after it was written.


NICT has two working prototypes. WISDOM-AR uses Japanese-language models and NICT's WISDOM X question-answering system to generate large numbers of high-risk prompts, then judges whether each answer is discriminatory, offensive or helps commit a crime, and explains the verdict. In one test an open model wrote a fraudulent investment email posing as a public authority. WISDOM-CE checks answers against large volumes of Japanese web pages to flag ハルシネーション (harushinēshon, hallucination) and bias; its demo caught a foreign model contradicting the Momotaro folk tale. NICT and PFN built a 200-billion-parameter model for the work from about 20 years of Japanese web data NICT has collected. PFN's PLaMo 3.0 Prime, on sale since June 22, came out of that joint research and will be plugged into the platform.

Investor takeaway: Sakura Internet (TSE: 3778) gains a service it can sell to tenants: a cloud where hosted models are tested against Japan-specific risks before customers buy them, which helps fill subsidized GPU capacity that still needs paying users. PFN gets a public reference point for PLaMo ahead of its planned 2028-2030 IPO. Watch whether the Digital Agency or the AI Safety Institute adopts the platform's scores, and whether NICT's FY2027 budget request funds it.

STORY 2 · SEMICONDUCTORS
CoreWeave's Bond Slide Knocks 3,318 Points Off the Nikkei and Advantest Falls 15%, on the Day TSMC Says Demand Still Outruns Supply

The Nikkei 225 fell 3,318 points, or 4.9%, to 64,425.95 on October 15, the third-largest 下げ幅 (sagehaba, size of decline) in points on record. The trigger came from US credit markets. CoreWeave, which rents Nvidia GPU capacity to AI developers and cloud providers, saw its 2032 bonds fall below 80 cents on the dollar on October 14, lifting the yield to about 14.8%. It carried $52 billion of liabilities including leases at the end of June, and its quarterly net interest bill more than doubled from a year earlier to $640 million. Its shares fell 15% in New York, the Philadelphia semiconductor index lost more than 4%, and September US consumer prices rose 3.7%, which raised bets on higher US rates. Turnover on Tokyo's Prime market reached ¥10.1 trillion.


Japan's chip-equipment makers took the heaviest losses. アドバンテスト (Adobantesuto, Advantest) fell 14.96% to ¥34,340 and by itself removed about 1,300 points from the index, because the price-weighted Nikkei gives 値がさ株 (negasa-kabu, high-priced shares) outsized influence. SUMCO fell 13.85%, レーザーテック (Rēzātekku, Lasertec) 13.56% and 国際電気 (Kokusai Denki, Kokusai Electric) 13.48%. 東京エレクトロン (Tōkyō Erekutoron, Tokyo Electron (TEL)) was down 7.3% and ソフトバンクグループ (Sofutobanku Gurūpu, SoftBank Group) 9.4% at the midday break. リスク回避 (risuku kaihi, risk aversion) pushed money into drugmakers, and pharmaceuticals rose 3.4% as a sector. Nomura strategist Wataru Akiyama said worries about the financial health of AI-related companies had risen quickly.

Investor takeaway: Tokyo's chip-tool stocks now trade on the credit of US data-center builders as well as on chip orders. TSMC's capex plan keeps tool orders intact for 2026, so the immediate damage is to valuations. The larger risk is that GPU cloud operators lose access to debt and cut purchases, which would hit Advantest's tester volumes first. Watch CoreWeave's bond prices, Tokyo Electron's results on October 30, and the trading level of SoftBank Group's 9.75% notes.

STORY 3 · MACRO
Core Machinery Orders Run 18.6% Above Last Year as the BOJ's Koeda Says There Is No Need to Be Overly Cautious on Hikes

Core machinery orders, the 先行指標 (senkō shihyō, leading indicator) for business capital spending, rose 5.2% in August from July to ¥1,069.8 billion, the 内閣府 (Naikakufu, Cabinet Office) said on October 15. Forecasts were for a gain of about 2% to 3% after July's 3.7% fall. The series tracks 民需 (minju, private-sector demand) and leaves out volatile orders for ships and electric power. Orders were 18.6% higher than a year earlier, the fastest annual pace in six months, and the three-month average rose 3.6%. The Cabinet Office kept its 基調判断 (kichō handan, overall assessment) that orders show signs of picking up.


Manufacturers ordered 1.6% more, at ¥527.0 billion, with non-ferrous metals up 132.6% and autos and parts up 13.1%. Non-manufacturers ordered 2.9% more, at ¥541.3 billion, led by goods leasing (up 136%) and wholesale and retail (up 29%). Leasing orders swing widely from month to month, so part of August's gain may reverse. Daiwa Institute of Research expects a gradual uptrend because companies keep spending on 省力化投資 (shōryokuka tōshi, labor-saving investment) to cope with staff shortages and on AI. It named a worse Middle East situation and raw material costs as the main downside risks.

Investor takeaway: Orders running 18.6% above last year give the BOJ evidence that companies can absorb another rate increase, and Koeda's remarks show at least one board member ready to vote for one. Automation suppliers such as Fanuc and Yaskawa gain if the orders turn into spending, while a higher policy rate and firmer yen trim exporters' yen-translated profits. Watch whether September orders hold the gain and how the BOJ revises its price forecasts at the October 29-30 meeting.
