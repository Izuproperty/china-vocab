STORY 1 · AI & TECH
SoftBank Weighs an $18 Billion Bid for Allied Universal to Put Robots on America's Largest Guard Force

ソフトバンクグループ (Sofutobanku Gurūpu, SoftBank Group) is in early talks to buy Allied Universal, the largest company in the US 警備業 (keibi-gyō, security services industry), Bloomberg reported on October 16. Reuters, citing a person familiar with the matter, said a deal could value Allied at about $18 billion, and The Wall Street Journal reported on October 17 that the figure of up to $18 billion excludes Allied's debt. Allied is owned by Warburg Pincus and Canadian pension fund La Caisse, operates in more than 100 countries and lists about $23 billion of annual revenue on its website. It bought British guard company G4S for about £3.8 billion in 2021. SoftBank declined to comment, and no price or financing has been agreed.


People familiar with the talks said SoftBank would over time hand some guard work, such as patrols, camera monitoring and access control, to 警備ロボット (keibi robotto, security robots), with humanoid machines further out. Allied already sells AI-based monitoring, incident reporting and GPS tracking, and it staffs the construction and operation of data centers, so SoftBank would own a services customer inside the AI buildout it is financing. Founder 孫正義 (Son Masayoshi, Masayoshi Son) has been building a robotics business around the $5.4 billion purchase of ABB's robotics division agreed last year. The Financial Times reported on October 9 that he had asked Gulf investors for up to $100 billion for a vehicle that would buy established companies and run them with AI and robots. Allied is the first large target to surface for that plan.


Guarding is a payroll business with thin margins, and US guard companies face the same 人手不足 (hitode busoku, labor shortage) that is pushing Japanese factories toward automation. Every shift a machine takes over moves labor cost into profit, and that is the only way an $18 billion price for a guard company earns a return. SoftBank's own balance sheet is stretched. It has committed about $64.6 billion to OpenAI, sold $11 billion of high-yield bonds in September at coupons up to 9.75%, and its shares fell about 9% at one point on October 15 when CoreWeave's bonds slid. An equity check of this size plus Allied's debt would likely need the Gulf vehicle or other outside money. SoftBank Group closed at ¥4,944 on October 16, before the report.

Investor takeaway: SoftBank Group (TSE: 9984) would be paying for a guard company whose upside depends on robots it has not yet deployed at scale, and its Pepper robot was shelved after weak demand. The bull case is a large captive customer for SoftBank's robotics unit plus Allied's data center contracts. Watch whether Gulf partners such as MGX co-invest, which would keep the debt off the parent balance sheet, and whether SoftBank puts a number on the guard tasks it expects to automate.

STORY 2 · INDUSTRIAL
Toyota Will Put Its Arene Software in All 50 Models and 10 Million Cars a Year by 2030

トヨタ自動車 (Toyota Jidōsha, Toyota Motor) expects its Arene vehicle software platform to run in about 50 models and 10 million new vehicles a year by 2030, the company said on October 16, confirming a Nikkei report. Chief technology officer 中嶋裕樹 (Nakajima Hiroki, Hiroki Nakajima) laid out the plan at an investor briefing in Brussels the previous week. Arene is developed by Woven by Toyota and first shipped in the redesigned RAV4 in 2025. It turns each car into a ソフトウェア定義車両 (sofutowea teigi sharyō, software-defined vehicle), whose driver assistance, safety and entertainment functions can be changed through a 無線アップデート (musen appudēto, over-the-air update) after the sale.


Toyota plans to offer Arene to partner automakers by 2035 and later to robots and to communication devices mounted on homes and utility poles, according to the Nikkei. A car could switch on the house lights as it pulls in, or receive a signal from a pole that a pedestrian is crossing ahead. The money sits in what Toyota calls its バリューチェーン事業 (baryū chēn jigyō, value chain business): software updates, connected safety services and parts sold after the new car. Toyota wants operating profit from that business to reach ¥3 trillion by fiscal 2030, about 40% above the current level.


Toyota sells roughly 10 million vehicles a year, so the target puts Arene on essentially its whole output. Tesla and Chinese EV makers such as BYD already update cars over the air, and Volkswagen's software unit Cariad has struggled with delays and losses, which shows how hard the switch is for a legacy automaker. Toyota holds stakes in Subaru, Mazda and Suzuki, which gives Arene a ready list of partner customers after 2035. Software-defined cars also move control into fewer, more powerful central computers, raising chip content per vehicle for suppliers such as Renesas and Denso.

Investor takeaway: Toyota (TSE: 7203) wants recurring software and service profit from cars already on the road, and a ¥3 trillion after-sale target would make that business a far larger share of earnings than the market prices today. Software delivery is the execution risk: a slip in Arene delays every model and partner launch behind it. Watch first-half results in early November for Arene milestones and whether the ¥3 trillion goal moves into formal guidance.

STORY 3 · MACRO
Takaichi's Approval Falls Below 50% as Most Voters Say They Worry About Her Food Tax Cut

Prime Minister 高市早苗 (Takaichi Sanae, Sanae Takaichi) saw her 内閣支持率 (naikaku shiji-ritsu, cabinet approval rating) fall to 49.1% in a Kyodo News telephone poll taken October 17-18, the first reading below 50% since she took office in October 2025. Disapproval was 33%. Kyodo tied the drop partly to Farm Minister Kazuo Yana, who resigned on October 14 after reports that he had talked about cutting road funding for municipalities that did not back him; 57.7% of respondents said Takaichi replaced him too late. A Jiji Press poll published on October 15 put support at 42.4%, also a record low for her cabinet.


More than 65% of Kyodo's respondents said they were concerned about the plan to cut the 軽減税率 (keigen zeiritsu, reduced tax rate) on food from 8% to 1% for two years from April 2027, Takaichi's main campaign promise. The cabinet approved the bill on October 9 together with an income-based cash benefit for low- and middle-income earners, at a combined cost of about ¥10 trillion over two years, and the government wants it enacted before the Diet session ends on December 12. It still has no named 財源 (zaigen, funding source). Takaichi has ruled out 赤字国債 (akaji kokusai, deficit-financing bonds), Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama has pushed the funding decision into the fiscal 2027 budget due in late December, and Keidanren opposes a corporate tax increase. Takaichi calls the cut a bridge to a 給付付き税額控除 (kyūfu-tsuki zeigaku kōjo, refundable tax credit) for low earners planned from 2029.


Rahul Anand, the IMF's mission chief for Japan, called the temporary cut "a very welcome step" in comments reported on October 17 and praised the pledge not to borrow for it. He also said support at Japan's debt level should be targeted, temporary and budget-neutral, warned that higher long-term rates would raise debt-service costs, and said the BOJ would need to hike if a weaker yen pushed inflation up. The 10-year JGB yield has climbed from about 1.6% when the cut was first floated to around 3.1%, and interest costs for fiscal 2027 are projected at ¥16.59 trillion, ¥3.5 trillion more than this year. The bill was scheduled for lower house debate from Tuesday, October 20.

Investor takeaway: A weaker prime minister has less room to choose a painful revenue source such as ending special tax breaks or raising corporate tax, so the bill is likely to pass with the financing question pushed into the December budget. That keeps pressure on super-long JGB yields, and a consumption subsidy adds to demand the BOJ is already trying to cool. Watch the lower house debate for any named revenue source, demand at the next 20-year and 30-year JGB auctions, and whether the next round of polls drops further.
