STORY 1 · AI & TECH
Tokyo Asks Companies to Name the Rules That Stop Them Deploying AI Agents and Robots

The Cabinet Office, the デジタル庁 (Dejitaru-chō, Digital Agency) and METI said on October 2 that they will collect reports from companies on regulations that block or blunt the 社会実装 (shakai jissō, real-world deployment) of AI. Submissions open on October 19 and close at 5 p.m. on October 30 through a Cabinet Office web form. The call covers large language models, multimodal and agentic AI, and physical AI, which the government defines as AI robots, fully driverless vehicles and AI drones. It reaches below statutes and cabinet orders to rules, public notices and the 通達 (tsūtatsu, administrative circular) that ministries send to the industries they supervise. METI minister 赤澤亮正 (Akazawa Ryōsei, Ryosei Akazawa) announced it alongside Digital Minister Furukawa and regulatory reform minister Nakatsuka, and said METI will give 伴走支援 (bansō shien, hands-on support) to companies whose cases are picked up, using the gray-zone clarification and special-exemption schemes in the Industrial Competitiveness Enhancement Act.


This is a repeat call, and this round names agentic and physical AI explicitly. It follows the AI Basic Plan revision in July, which committed the government to rebuilding rules on the assumption that AI is in use. Results go to the 規制改革推進会議 (Kisei Kaikaku Suishin Kaigi, Regulatory Reform Promotion Council), into the next revision of the AI Basic Plan and into a cross-ministry review of rules. The timing lines up with the year-end AI action plan that Prime Minister Takaichi ordered on October 1, so the cases filed this month can shape what the cabinet adopts in December.

Investor takeaway: The call targets the circulars and notices that require a human in the loop, which is where Japanese AI agent and robot deployments actually stall. Cases filed by October 30 can enter Takaichi's year-end AI action plan. Watch the November case list and whether the FSA and health ministry set revision dates; IT services names with agent products benefit first.

STORY 2 · SEMICONDUCTORS
Rohm, Toshiba and Mitsubishi Electric Miss Their Summer Deadline on a Power-Chip Merger, and METI Steps In to Push

ローム (Rōmu, Rohm) (TSE: 6963), 東芝 (Tōshiba, Toshiba) and 三菱電機 (Mitsubishi Denki, Mitsubishi Electric) (TSE: 6503) have missed the summer target they set in March for agreeing to merge their パワー半導体 (pawā handōtai, power semiconductor) businesses. Jiji Press reported on September 30 that the three still favor a holding-company structure but cannot agree on the 出資比率 (shusshi hiritsu, equity ratio) in the new company, which business lines each side carves out, or who runs it. Jiji described the impasse as a 主導権争い (shudōken arasoi, fight for control). The companies now aim to settle terms around their November interim results, with March 2027 as a fallback, and any deal needs Chinese antitrust clearance.


On October 2 METI minister Ryosei Akazawa declined to comment on the talks but called power chips essential to everything from cars to AI data centers and said METI will back domestic collaboration and 再編 (saihen, realignment). The three bring different technology: Rohm makes 炭化ケイ素 (tanka keiso, silicon carbide (SiC)) devices, Toshiba's device unit makes silicon MOSFETs, and Mitsubishi Electric leads in IGBT modules for trains, industrial drives and EVs. Combined, they would rank second in the world behind Germany's Infineon. Denso withdrew its proposal to take a stake in Rohm on April 28, which removed one obstacle, and METI already subsidized a 2023 Rohm-Toshiba production tie-up with up to ¥129.4 billion.

Investor takeaway: Rohm, Toshiba and Mitsubishi Electric agree on merging but not on who owns and runs the merged power-chip company. METI is signaling that future subsidies favor the combined entity. The November interim results are the next deadline; Rohm (TSE: 6963) has the most riding on a signed ownership split.

STORY 3 · MACRO
Japan's 10-Year Bond Sale Draws Its Strongest Demand Since May as the Yield Hits 3.1%

The Ministry of Finance sold ¥2.6 trillion of new 10-year JGBs on October 6 with a 3.1% coupon, up from 2.7% on the previous issue. The 落札利回り (rakusatsu rimawari, accepted auction yield) at the lowest price was 3.103%. Bids totaled ¥7.4 trillion for ¥1.97 trillion of competitive allotment, a bid-to-cover of 3.76 against 3.29 in September and a 12-month average of 3.21, the strongest result since May. The tail, the gap between the average and lowest accepted prices, narrowed to 0.02 yen from 0.12. Bond futures recovered their morning losses after the result, and the 10-year yield closed near 3.1% after touching 3.115%, matching its highest level since 1996.


The long end stayed under pressure. The 20-year yield reached 3.99%, a three-decade high, and the 30-year held near its record of 4.235% set on October 5. That was the day Prime Minister Takaichi told the Diet she would "control" bond issuance and that fiscal sustainability is a prerequisite for her "responsible and proactive" fiscal policy, while still pushing a two-year food tax cut from April and multi-year spending on economic security. On October 6 Governor Ueda said the BOJ will keep raising rates if its forecasts hold, and USD/JPY ended near ¥158.2.

Investor takeaway: Domestic investors bought the 10-year JGB at 3.1% but still avoid 20- and 30-year paper while Takaichi's spending plans lack funding. The October 8 30-year auction is the next test; a weak result steepens the curve, which supports bank net interest margins and pressures life insurers. Higher JGB yields also reduce Japanese demand for foreign bonds.
