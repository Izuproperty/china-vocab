STORY 1 · AI & TECH
Nippan Admits Selling Books to Anthropic, and Japan's Publishers Want the AI Supply Shut Off

日販グループホールディングス (Nippan Gurūpu Hōrudingusu, Nippan Group Holdings), one of Japan's two dominant 取次 (toritsugi, book wholesalers), has confirmed that a group company sold large volumes of Japanese books to US AI developer アンソロピック (Ansoropikku, Anthropic) between December 2024 and December 2025. The Yomiuri Shimbun reported the admission on October 6, and Nikkei reported on October 7 that Nippan is now answering inquiries from individual publishers about which titles were included. The sales surfaced in internal Anthropic documents filed in a US authors' class action, which listed "Nippan Japan" among suppliers to a book-buying program. Nippan says it did not know the books were bought for AI training. It has not disclosed how many volumes were sold or at what price.


The 日本書籍出版協会 (Nihon Shoseki Shuppan Kyōkai, Japan Book Publishers Association), which represents about 380 publishers, wrote to Nippan on September 17 asking for the facts and for an explanation of how a bulk sale to an overseas buyer fits the 再販制度 (saihan seido, resale price maintenance system), under which publishers fix retail prices and wholesalers agree to sell at them. After the admission, the association asked Nippan to stop supplying books for AI training, and one publishing executive told the Yomiuri the industry "cannot forgive" it. US court records describe Anthropic buying print books, cutting off the spines and scanning the pages, and publishers fear Japanese titles went through the same process.

Investor takeaway: Japanese copyright law allows AI training, so publishers are fighting over supply contracts instead. Tighter wholesale terms would push AI developers toward paid licences with publishers and raise the cost of Japanese training text. Watch Tohan's response and any publisher compensation claims.

STORY 2 · SEMICONDUCTORS
Tokyo Electron's CFO Says Price Increases Will Help Lift Operating Profit to a Record ¥1 Trillion

東京エレクトロン (Tōkyō Erekutoron, Tokyo Electron, TEL) (TSE: 8035) expects 営業利益 (eigyō rieki, operating profit) to rise about 60% to a record ¥1 trillion in the year ending March 2027, chief financial officer 川本弘 (Kawamoto Hiroshi) told Nikkei Asia in an interview published on October 6. He attributed the increase to strong equipment sales and to 値上げ (neage, price increases), and said TEL is working to raise the value of its tools as chipmakers' qualification testing gets stricter. In July TEL raised first-half guidance to ¥1.62 trillion of sales and ¥458 billion of operating profit, so the ¥1 trillion target requires about ¥542 billion in the 下期 (shimoki, second half), an 18% step up from the first half.


TEL has raised its forecast for 前工程装置 (maekōtei sōchi, front-end wafer fab equipment) spending to more than $150 billion in 2026 and more than $190 billion in 2027. It expects second-quarter new-equipment sales of ¥708.7 billion, a quarterly record and 33% above the first quarter. AI data centers drive the orders: GPU and high-bandwidth memory production first, and now commodity DRAM and server CPUs as well. Samsung reported record quarterly profit on October 7 on higher memory prices, which gives TEL's largest memory customers the cash to keep ordering.

Investor takeaway: TEL's path to ¥1 trillion depends on a second half about 18% larger than the first and on price increases that lift gross margin toward 50%. Memory capex is the swing factor. The October 30 results will show whether orders back the plan.

STORY 3 · MACRO
Record ¥5.1 Trillion of Overseas Income Carries Japan's Current Account Past a Goods Deficit

The 財務省 (Zaimushō, Ministry of Finance) said on October 8 that Japan's 経常収支 (keijō shūshi, current account) surplus was ¥4.062 trillion in August, well above the ¥3.19 trillion median forecast, up about 12% from a year earlier and the 19th straight monthly surplus. The 第一次所得収支 (daiichiji shotoku shūshi, primary income balance), which records dividends and interest earned abroad, hit a record ¥5.133 trillion, up 21.6% and the highest since the series began in 1985. The goods 貿易収支 (bōeki shūshi, trade balance) swung to a ¥687.7 billion deficit from a ¥113.8 billion surplus, as imports rose 26.7% to ¥10.55 trillion on energy and raw material costs while exports rose 16.9% to ¥9.86 trillion. The services deficit narrowed to ¥115.8 billion from ¥266.7 billion as inbound tourist spending grew.


A weaker yen did much of the work. The currency was about 7.5% weaker against the dollar than a year earlier, which raised the yen value of profits from overseas subsidiaries and coupons on foreign bonds. Japan now earns its surplus from returns on past investment abroad and runs a deficit on goods, because high oil prices are inflating the import bill. Much of that income never comes home: companies reinvest subsidiary profits locally and investors keep coupons in foreign currency, so a record primary-income surplus creates far less yen buying than an equal trade surplus would.

Investor takeaway: Japan's surplus now comes from overseas investment income, much of which stays abroad, so the record does little to support the yen near ¥158. Energy imports keep the goods balance in deficit. The weak yen keeps import inflation in front of the BOJ at its October 29-30 meeting.
