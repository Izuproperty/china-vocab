STORY 1 · AI & TECH
Tokyo Plans a Security Certificate for Data Centers and Clouds That Would Keep Chinese Providers Out

The 総務省 (Sōmushō, Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications) and 経済産業省 (Keizai Sangyō-shō, METI) plan a national 認証制度 (ninshō seido, certification system) for data center operators and cloud providers, the Nikkei reported on October 19. It would cover companies that run data centers in Japan and those that sell cloud services from servers in Japan. Assessors would review each operator's business details, its safeguards against data leaks and the suppliers of its equipment. Banks, hospitals and other holders of sensitive personal data would be told to use certified services. Use would be voluntary, and the system would start after fiscal 2027.


The enforcement power sits in a separate bill. The government plans to send an amendment to the 経済安全保障推進法 (Keizai Anzen Hoshō Suishin-hō, Economic Security Promotion Act) to the ordinary Diet session that opens in January. It would require companies that store or provide important personal data to notify the government in advance, and it would let the government issue recommendations or suspension orders when it judges that the data is stored in a risky way. The certification criteria would build on JC-STAR, the IoT security label run by the 情報処理推進機構 (Jōhō Shori Suishin Kikō, IPA, Information-technology Promotion Agency) under METI. JC-STAR has no rule that excludes any country, yet it has certified no Chinese product so far. The Nikkei pointed to China's 国家情報法 (Kokka Jōhō-hō, National Intelligence Law), which can oblige Chinese companies to cooperate with state intelligence work even when their servers are in Japan. From April 2027, grid-connected solar equipment and storage batteries must also meet JC-STAR Level 1.


Japan's cloud market passed ¥1.7 trillion in 2024, according to the Cabinet Secretariat, and generative AI is pushing more sensitive data into it. A voluntary label still decides purchases in regulated industries, because a bank that picks an uncertified provider will have to justify the choice to its supervisor once the government starts examining data holders directly. Alibaba Cloud, which has sold Japanese data center capacity since 2016, is the provider with the most to lose. Domestic operators that market sovereign capacity, such as さくらインターネット (Sakura Intānetto, Sakura Internet) and KDDI, gain a certificate to show customers. The report did not say how Amazon and Microsoft, the two largest providers in Japan, would be assessed.

Investor takeaway: Sakura Internet (TSE: 3778) and other domestic GPU-cloud operators get a sales argument with financial and medical customers, but certification starts after fiscal 2027 and use is voluntary, so revenue effects are years out. The January bill acts sooner, since prior notification and suspension orders bind the data holders themselves. Watch the bill text for how it defines foreign control, and whether the Japan regions of US hyperscalers seek certification early.

STORY 2 · SEMICONDUCTORS
METI Clears TSMC to Make 3nm Chips in Kumamoto From 2028 and Trims the Subsidy to ¥645 Billion

経済産業省 (Keizai Sangyō-shō, METI) on October 16 approved a revised plan from TSMC and its Japanese unit JASM for the 熊本第2工場 (Kumamoto Dai-ni Kōjō, Kumamoto second fab) in 菊陽町 (Kikuyō-machi, Kikuyo Town). The fab will make 3-nanometer logic chips, the first 3nm production in Japan, with first shipments due by December 2028. The approved first phase has a 月産 (gessan, monthly output) of 6,000 wafers. The original plan called for 6nm to 12nm chips plus 40nm, at 48,000 wafers a month.


METI cut the maximum subsidy for the second fab from ¥732 billion to ¥645.15 billion (about $4.1 billion) to match the smaller first phase, and it will request more money in the fiscal 2027 budget if TSMC adds capacity. Investment in the second fab is expected to exceed $15.7 billion, and TSMC puts total spending on both Kumamoto fabs above $23 billion. Counting the first fab, public support could pass ¥1 trillion. METI also approved a smaller plan for the first fab, which drops its 12-16nm process and will run 45,000 wafers a month of 22-28nm chips, down from 55,000 in the earlier plan. Kumamoto Governor 木村敬 (Kimura Kei, Kei Kimura) welcomed the decision.


A 3nm wafer sells for several times the price of a 28nm wafer, so 6,000 wafers a month can bring in more revenue than far larger volumes at older nodes. The approval gives Japan a domestic source of 先端半導体 (sentan handōtai, leading-edge chips) for JASM's minority owners Sony, Denso and Toyota, and for Japanese AI chip designers that now wait for capacity in Taiwan. Rapidus, which targets 2nm mass production in Hokkaido in 2027 and signed 17 design houses this month to find customers, will now compete with TSMC for the same Japanese design wins.

Investor takeaway: Tokyo Electron (TSE: 8035) and the materials and gas suppliers around Kumamoto now have a dated 3nm tool schedule running to late 2028, though a 6,000-wafer phase means a smaller equipment order than the old 48,000-wafer plan implied. The fiscal 2027 budget request will show whether METI is ready to fund a second phase. Watch the December budget line for Kumamoto and any TSMC comment on which Japanese customers have booked 3nm capacity.

STORY 3 · FINANCE
Dai-ichi Life Offers A$7.9 Billion for Challenger, Australia's Largest Annuity Seller

第一生命グループ (Daiichi Seimei Gurūpu, Dai-ichi Life Group) made a 買収提案 (baishū teian, takeover proposal) for Challenger, Australia's largest seller of 年金保険 (nenkin hoken, annuity products), through its local unit TAL Dai-ichi Life Australia. The indicative, non-binding offer of A$11.80 a share values Challenger at about A$7.94 billion (US$5.5 billion), a 19.8% premium to the October 16 close of A$9.85. Dai-ichi already owns 20.36%. It bought its first 15.1% from MS&AD Insurance Group in April 2025 at about A$8.46 a share, so the new price is nearly 40% higher. Challenger shares rose as much as 16.7% on October 19 to A$11.49, the highest since October 2018. Challenger's board said it is considering the offer, which depends on due diligence, a binding scheme agreement, regulatory approval and a shareholder vote.


Challenger turns retirement savings into guaranteed income. Australia's compulsory superannuation system has built large savings pools that members are now starting to draw down, and annuities are the product that converts a lump sum into a pension. Challenger's assets under management rose 5% in the July-September quarter to A$31.9 billion, and total sales rose 52% from a year earlier to A$4.4 billion. In June it agreed to merge its Fidante fund-management arm into Channel Capital, leaving a business built around retirement income, which is the part Dai-ichi wants.


人口減少 (jinkō genshō, population decline) shrinks the market for life insurance in Japan, so Dai-ichi has been buying growth abroad: it already owns TAL in Australia and Protective Life in the US. The bid comes as yields at home rise. The 10-year JGB was 2.91% on October 19, more than a point above a year earlier, which lifts the return Japanese insurers can earn on yen bonds without leaving the country. Dai-ichi is still choosing to put roughly A$6.3 billion into the shares it does not own, because higher domestic yields do nothing to replace the premiums it loses as its customer base ages and shrinks.

Investor takeaway: Dai-ichi Life (TSE: 8750) would add a growing annuity book outside Japan for about US$4.4 billion, paid at a price well above its own entry point last year. Challenger's board may push for more, and the funding mix of cash against hybrid debt will matter for Dai-ichi's capital ratio. Watch for a board recommendation backed by a signed scheme implementation agreement, and for overseas bids from other Japanese life insurers.
